## Part 1: Cold open: Pump Four  (795 characters)

```text
At two-thirteen in the morning, the bell rings at pump four.

That bell means somebody just lifted a nozzle. I look up from the register. There's no car at pump four. There's a man. He's holding the nozzle against his chest like he's hugging it, and he's staring at the store. Not at me. At the glass.

I don't move. My headlamp is off. The store lights are off. Out there, under the canopy, it's bright as noon.

He leans closer. His nose touches the window. And all he sees… is himself.

After a while, he lets go of the nozzle and walks back to the highway, to join the others.

There are always others.

My name is Sam. I'm nineteen. I work the night shift at Sunny's, mile eighty-eight on Route 9. The last gas station before the desert. This is night thirty. Nobody has come to relieve me.
```

## Part 2: Chapter 1: Last Car Out  (1513 characters)
Save as **part2.mp3**

```text
It started on a Tuesday. The radio said stay indoors. Then the radio said a lot of things. Then it just played the same emergency tone, over and over.

Taped to my register is Mr. Okafor's night-shift card. Ten rules. Nine of them are about shoplifters and bathroom keys. Rule ten is in red marker, and he never told me what it means.

By midnight, cars were flying past at a hundred miles an hour. All of them heading east, away from the city.

One pulled in. A blue hatchback, smoking. A woman jumped out with a little girl in pajamas and a backpack shaped like a frog.

"Gas," she said. "Please. Anything."

I switched on pump three. She was filling up when the truck came. A big rig, swerving across both lanes. The driver was slumped against his window. The truck clipped the island and tore pump three clean off its base. Gasoline sprayed across the concrete like a fire hose.

I knew exactly what to do, because Mr. Okafor made me practice it on my first day. Every gas station has a big red emergency stop button. Press it, and every pump on the lot loses power at once.

I slammed it. The spraying stopped.

Then the truck door opened, and the driver climbed down. He wasn't hurt. He wasn't anything. His head turned, slowly, toward the little girl.

The woman shoved her daughter through my door. "Her name is Junie. Keep her inside. Keep the lights on. I'll find you."

And she ran for the hatchback, honking and waving, leading him away down the dark road.

That was the last car that ever drove east.
```

## Part 3: Chapter 2: The Walk / Chapter 3: Water  (1682 characters)
Save as **part3.mp3**

```text
On the second day, they came on foot.

Not running. Walking. Down the middle of Route 9, all heading west, toward the city. Truckers. Hikers. A whole wedding party, bow ties still on. Thousands of them. They didn't stop. They didn't look at anything.

Junie called it the Walk.

And every night, a few of them would drift off the road, under the canopy lights, and come right up to our windows.

The first time, I almost screamed. Junie grabbed my sleeve and pointed. The dead man outside was lifting his hand to his head, over and over, like he was fixing his hair in a mirror.

Because he was.

Here's something true. At night, when it's bright outside and dark inside, a window turns into a mirror for whoever's on the bright side. From out there, they can't see in. They only see the canopy, the pumps… and themselves.

So we made our own rule one. Outside lights on. Inside lights off. Always.

We lived in the dark. We whispered. We ate gummy bears and beef jerky by the light leaking in from the canopy. Junie counted them walking past. She stopped at four thousand.

Food was never the problem. A gas station is basically a castle made of snacks.

Water was the problem.

You need about one gallon of water per person, every day. Drinking and washing. I counted every bottle in the walk-in cooler. Two people. Forty-one days. If we were careful.

The walk-in became our bedroom. It's the only room with no windows. Junie was scared of the heavy door at first, so I showed her the big red knob on the inside. Walk-in coolers are built with a safety release, so you can always push your way out. Nobody gets locked in.

She pressed it twenty times that night. Just to be sure.
```

## Part 4: Chapter 4: The Lights Go Out / Chapter 5: Twenty Thousand Gallons  (1837 characters)
Save as **part4.mp3**

```text
On night ten, the power died.

The canopy went black. The whole highway went black. And for the first time, from the outside, our windows were just… windows.

I grabbed Junie, and we ran for the cooler. Behind us, something tapped the glass. Then something else. Then the whole front of the store was tapping.

Mr. Okafor had a backup generator out back. I'd never started it. It needed gasoline.

And I was sitting on top of twenty thousand gallons of it.

That's the joke about gas stations. The fuel lives underground, in giant tanks. The pumps that bring it up need electricity. No power, no pumps. You can be standing on an ocean of gasoline and not have a single cup.

There's one other way into the tank: a metal cap in the concrete called the fill port. It's where the tanker truck delivers. Mr. Okafor kept an old hand pump in the back for emergencies. I always thought it was junk.

At four in the morning, I crawled out the back door with a red gas can, the hand pump, and my headlamp switched off.

The fill port is twenty feet from the highway. The Walk was still going past. Close enough to hear their feet.

I pumped. Slowly. Every squeak of that handle sounded like a car alarm.

Here's another true thing. When you fill a gas can, you set it on the ground. Never in a truck bed. Never in your lap. Static electricity can build up, and one spark near gas fumes is all it takes.

I set the can on the concrete. I pumped until it was heavy.

Then I heard footsteps on the gravel. Not out on the road. Behind me.

I didn't turn around. I picked up the can and walked to the back door, one step at a time, matching my steps to the Walk, so mine wouldn't stand out.

Junie had the door open an inch. I slid in. She shut it.

Something leaned on that door from the outside for a long, long time.

Then it went back to the road.
```

## Part 5: Chapter 6: Keep the Lights On / Chapter 7: The Wrong Way  (1572 characters)
Save as **part5.mp3**

```text
The generator coughed, and then roared. The canopy lights flickered back on. Outside, every face at the glass turned back into a reflection.

After that, I refilled the generator every third night. Junie timed the gaps in the Walk with a stopwatch and tapped my shoulder when it was safe. We got good at it.

But she asked me the same thing every night. "Is the city far?"

Forty miles, I told her.

"Can my mom see our lights from there?"

I didn't know. Out here there's no other light for forty miles. Just us. I told her yes.

So every night, she stood at the window, inside, in the dark, and waved at the road.

Night thirty. The bell rings at pump four. That's where I started this story.

After the man at pump four walked back to the road, Junie grabbed my arm.

"Sam. Look."

Far down the highway, in the middle of the Walk, one person was going the wrong way. East. Against all of them. Toward us.

She was limping. Something green was tied around her wrist like a bandage. A strap from a frog-shaped backpack.

The dead didn't look at her, because she walked the way they walked. Slow. Shuffling. Matching their steps.

She crossed the lot. She put both hands on the glass.

And Junie, who had followed rule one for thirty nights, reached up and flipped the inside lights on.

For one second, the window stopped being a mirror. The woman saw her daughter. Her daughter saw her.

And every one of the dead outside saw them too.

I hit the lock, dragged the door open, and pulled her in by her jacket. We ran for the cooler. Behind us, the glass started to shake.
```

## Part 6: Ending: The Only Light  (755 characters)
Save as **part6.mp3**

```text
In the cooler, in the dark, Junie's mom held her and didn't let go.

"I saw your lights," she whispered. "From the hills. It's the only light left for forty miles."

Then she told me the part she didn't want Junie to hear.

The city is dark now. All of it. And tonight, the Walk stopped going west. Because there's nothing left out there to walk toward.

So they're turning around.

Through the cooler door, I can hear it. Thousands of feet on the highway, slowing down. Stopping. Turning toward Sunny's.

Toward the only light left.

I have one gas can. And taped to the cooler wall is Mr. Okafor's card. Rule ten, in red marker:

If they ever all come at once, don't hide the light. Use it.

I don't know what that means yet.

But I'm about to find out.
```
