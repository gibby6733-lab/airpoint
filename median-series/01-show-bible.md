# MEDIAN — Show Bible (v1.0)

> *"America tries it here first."*

---

## 0. The pitch in one breath

In 1987 a market research firm found that one small Nebraska town matched the demographics of the United States almost exactly. Ever since, **Median, Nebraska** has been America's official test market. Every new snack, app, AI, political slogan, wellness trend and terrible idea lands here first. If Median likes it, the whole country gets it six months later. If Median hates it, it gets buried on Recall Hill.

Six seventh graders grow up inside the experiment. The adults are paid to be average, and the kids keep accidentally deciding what the rest of the country will believe next.

**Why this premise works for a long-running satire show:**
- **A built-in story engine.** Every new trend in the real world can arrive in Median as a "test." You never run out of topics.
- **Satire with a reason.** The town isn't randomly crazy. It's crazy because corporations, politicians and algorithms are experimenting on it.
- **Stakes that scale.** What the kids do here really does spread to the whole country, so a playground scheme can become a national crisis in one episode.
- **A clear difference from South Park.** It isn't a mountain town where weird things happen. It's a laboratory town where weird things get *shipped* to everyone else.

## 2. The town: Median, Nebraska

### Vital statistics
- **Population:** 4,812, and the town sign updates in real time. Keeping that number "statistically perfect" is a civic obsession. A recurring background gag: when a baby is born, someone is quietly paid to move away.
- **Founded:** 1871 by surveyor **Elias Thornbury**, who got tired halfway across the state, said *"This'll do,"* and sat down. His statue is a man sitting on a rock looking vaguely disappointed.
- **Motto:** *"Neither Here Nor There."*
- **School mascot:** The Median Moderates, nicknamed **"The Fightin' Moderates."** The mascot is a beige foam blob in a sweater vest called **Moe Derate**.
- **Economy:** Most households receive a monthly **Participation Stipend** from Aggregate Insights for taking part in product tests. Being average *is* the local industry. Being too weird, too rich, too smart or too poor is a financial threat to the whole town, so conformity is enforced with a smile.

### The history in four beats (told on screen as a 1970s-style "Median Minute" filmstrip)
1. **1871:** Thornbury stops in the middle of nowhere. Nobody follows him for eleven years.
2. **1987:** Aggregate Insights finds that Median matches U.S. census averages "to the decimal." A black glass cube appears in a cornfield overnight.
3. **1994:** The **Self-Heating Soup Incident.** A test product called *SoupStart™*, a fictional self-heating soup can, causes a fire that destroys Old Downtown. The crater, now called **The Pit**, is fenced off and has a plaque.
4. **Today:** Median has been the national test market for nearly four decades. Residents have tried 31,000 products, 9 failed currencies and 3 pilot versions of daylight saving time.

### Strange local traditions (each one is a future episode)
- **Average Day (every October):** The town is weighed, measured and surveyed in a parade. One family is crowned **"The Median Family"** and rides on a float. Being chosen is an honor and also a curse.
- **The Burning of the Returns (New Year's Eve):** Residents throw the year's recalled products into a bonfire at the base of Recall Hill and sing the town song. Some of the products are not fully dead.
- **Focus Fridays:** Free sandwiches and $40 for trying something behind the mirror. Attendance is technically voluntary.
- **The Recall Siren:** A civil-defense siren followed by a calm PA voice: *"Attention, Median. Please return your [product]. Do not eat, wear or emotionally bond with it."* (This is the show's signature sound cue.)
- **Thornbury Sit-Down (every spring):** At noon the whole town sits down for one minute wherever they are, to honor the founder giving up.

## 3. The main cast: six characters

> All six are **seventh graders (ages 12–13)** at Median Middle School. This age is deliberate: they're old enough to have phones, side hustles and opinions, and young enough to be dumb about all of them.

### 3.1 DEX PRUITT: "The Visionary"

**Archetype starting point:** the overconfident idiot who thinks he's a genius.

**Personality:** Dex has watched every startup keynote ever posted. He talks like a founder giving a product launch, and he has none of the substance. He's not stupid so much as *untroubled by thinking*. He grabs an idea, names it and announces it before anyone can check whether it works. He's very loyal and gives fully to every plan, which is exactly why his plans get so far.

**History and depth:** Dex's dad, **Rick Pruitt**, left two years ago to "find himself" and now hosts a men's-wellness podcast with 300 listeners called *Unshackled*. Dex has never admitted it hurts. His overconfidence is armor: if he's always "about to change the world," he never has to feel left behind. His mom Gail overcompensates by buying him things.

**Ambition:** To be on the cover of a magazine before he's 14, any magazine.

**Strengths:** fearless, a natural showman, makes people *want* to follow him, never discouraged.
**Weaknesses:** zero follow-through, can't take criticism, claims credit for everything, and doesn't read terms and conditions.
**Comedy engine:** the gap between how big he talks and how small the plan is.

**Voice and speech:**
- Long pauses for applause that doesn't come.
- Invents named concepts: *"This is what I call… the Pruitt Principle."* Every Pruitt Principle is something obvious.
- Says "Think about it" right before something nobody should think about.
- Mangles business words ("We need to synergize the disruption vertically").
- When he's caught out, he switches to "I was testing you."
- **Voice casting note:** a bright, slightly too-loud early-teen boy. Has the cadence of a confident product presenter, with a voice that cracks on high notes.

**Recurring jokes:** the headset mic that isn't plugged into anything; "Pruitt Labs" painted on the garage; asking Alexa-style speakers for feedback; the Pruitt Principles (numbered, and the numbers don't go in order).

---

### 3.2 WREN OKAFOR: "The Only Sane One (She Thinks)"

**Archetype starting point:** the sarcastic friend who sees through everyone's nonsense.

**Personality:** Dry, fast and observant. Wren is the audience's way in: she says what we're thinking. She isn't cruel; she's tired of watching the whole town fall for things. Her cynicism is usually right, which makes it funny when it's *wrong*. Some of the show's best episodes end with Wren being too cynical about something that turns out to be sincere. (That's the main lesson from the reference episode: the cynic doesn't always win.)

**History and depth:** She lives above **Spin Cycle Laundromat** in Quartile Acres with her mom, **Adaeze Okafor**, who runs it and takes night classes in accounting. Her dad is an over-the-road trucker who calls on Sundays. Wren reads everything and has an escape plan (a college scholarship, then a big city, then "anywhere with a decent library"). **Secret:** she's obsessed with **Moonblade Academy**, a cheesy fantasy book series about wizard teenagers with destinies, the kind of thing she mocks in public. She has fan art. She has a cosplay. Nobody can ever find out.

**Ambition:** To get out of Median, and secretly to belong somewhere.

**Strengths:** smart, honest, the best judge of character in the group, can't be hypnotized by hype.
**Weaknesses:** smug, assumes the worst about people, avoids feelings, and is very hypocritical about Moonblade.
**Comedy engine:** deadpan reactions, being the straight woman to everyone else, and the occasional public collapse when her secret leaks out.

**Voice and speech:**
- Short sentences, and often one word: "No." "Cool." "Incredible."
- Asks follow-up questions that expose a plan's flaws: "And then what?"
- Stays calm when other characters scream. That's the joke.
- When she does lose it, it's a perfectly organized rant with numbered points.
- **Voice casting note:** a low, dry, unimpressed early-teen girl's voice, with perfect timing on silences.

**Recurring jokes:** wearing headphones she isn't listening to anything on, so people leave her alone; the fact-check face (one eyebrow); Moonblade near-misses; "I want it on the record that I said no."

---

### 3.3 MARCUS "MARGIN" FELD: "The Hustler"

**Archetype starting point:** the greedy entrepreneur always chasing money.

**Personality:** Marcus has had an LLC since he was ten. He sees every situation as a market. He resells cafeteria snacks, rents out his locker and once sold "premium" seats on the school bus. He talks like a finance guy who has watched too much business TV. He isn't evil, just *incentivized*. If you make it worth his while, he'll do anything, including the right thing.

**History and depth:** His mom, **Denise Feld**, works three jobs, and his dad's medical bills nearly bankrupted the family before he died when Marcus was eight. Marcus's greed is fear: if he has enough money, nothing bad can happen again. He keeps a ledger of every dollar and every favor. On the last page is a figure he's saving toward: **the exact amount of his mom's remaining debt.** That's the show's emotional ace, revealed in the Season 1 finale.

**Ambition:** To be a self-made millionaire by 18 (he has a countdown widget).

**Strengths:** quick at numbers, persuasive, practical, the only one who reads contracts.
**Weaknesses:** sells out his friends at the slightest temptation, can't relax, sees people as assets.
**Comedy engine:** turning every emotional moment into a transaction ("I'm sorry for your loss. Have you thought about the resale value of the casket?").

**Voice and speech:**
- Finance slang: "basis points," "liquidity," "let's circle back," "that's a revenue problem."
- Negotiates everything, even apologies: "I'll give you 60% of a sorry."
- Talks fast, especially when lying.
- Has a short laugh when he smells money, like a cash register.
- **Voice casting note:** a nasal, quick-talking young voice in the style of a used-car salesman.

**Recurring jokes:** the fanny pack full of cash; the ledger; the countdown widget; the rivalry with Bryce Ellington-Shaw IV; charging the gang "a friend rate" (which is higher).

---

### 3.4 GORDY BAKSHI: "The Guy Who's Right for the Wrong Reasons"

**Archetype starting point:** the socially awkward conspiracy theorist.

**Personality:** Gordy is sure everything is connected. The twist is that in Median, things *are*. The town really is being watched and experimented on. Gordy is the only one who sees it, but he always gets the details wildly wrong (he thinks Aggregate Insights is run by "lizard accountants"). He's awkward, loves deeply, and tells too much about himself to anyone who listens. He's the warm heart of the group, though nobody would say so.

**History and depth:** He lives with his dad, **Sanjay Bakshi**, a calm, sweet ham-radio enthusiast who is completely normal. That frustrates Gordy, who wants his dad to be a secret agent. Gordy's mom lives in Phoenix with a new family, and Gordy has decided she was "relocated by them." The real story is mundane and sad, and he isn't ready to hear it.

**Ambition:** To expose "The Truth" (he isn't sure what it is) and be believed for once.

**Strengths:** notices details nobody else does, brave in strange situations, endlessly loyal, a good researcher.
**Weaknesses:** no social filter, panics, jumps to conclusions, can't keep a secret.
**Comedy engine:** delivering absurd theories with total conviction, then turning out to be *partly right*.

**Voice and speech:**
- Talks fast, in numbered points, then loses count: "Point one. Point two. Point… also two."
- Catchphrase: **"I'm not saying it's connected. I'm saying it's adjacent."**
- Makes his own sound effects while explaining (whooshes, beeps).
- Over-shares at the worst moments: "Speaking of secret tunnels, I had a dream about my mom."
- **Voice casting note:** a high, breathless, nasal early-teen boy's voice that speeds up as he gets excited.

**Recurring jokes:** the bucket hat with a foil-colored lining ("it's not foil, it's a *blend*"); the lanyard with 12 keys, none to anything; the conspiracy corkboard in his bedroom; the ham-radio "contact" who is just his dad in the next room.

### 3.5 KENZIE VALE: "The Main Character"

**Archetype starting point:** the popular kid desperate for attention.

**Personality:** Kenzie narrates her life for an audience of 2,300 followers. She speaks in captions, reacts to everything at maximum volume and turns any event into content. She's quick, ambitious and more strategic than people realize. She understands attention better than anyone in town, including the corporations. Her flaw is that she doesn't know who she is when the camera is off.

**History and depth:** She lives in the Upper Quartile with very busy, very rich parents. Her twin **Kaylee** is effortlessly popular, isn't on social media, and doesn't care, which makes her more beloved. Kenzie is "the other Vale." Everything she does is an attempt to be seen apart from her sister. Underneath it she's lonely, and the gang is her first group of friends who aren't followers.

**Ambition:** One million followers, a brand deal, and her sister's quiet respect.

**Strengths:** charisma, media savvy, a fast talker, fearless in public.
**Weaknesses:** vain, shallow in a crisis, would trade a friend for a viral moment, can't stand being ignored.
**Comedy engine:** narrating disasters as content while they happen, and huge emotional swings that last seconds.

**Voice and speech:**
- Talks in captions: "Not me literally crying in the ValuBarn parking lot."
- Uses "literally" for things that are not literal.
- Turns to the camera mid-conversation to add context for her followers.
- Two voices: her **"camera voice"** (bright and fake) and her **real voice** (flat and sharp). The switch between them is the joke.
- **Voice casting note:** a bright, vocal-fry teen-girl voice with a sudden plummet to a normal register.

**Recurring jokes:** the follower counter shown as an on-screen graphic that ticks up and down; the phone ring light; being mistaken for Kaylee; "This is going in the vlog," always at the worst moment.

---

### 3.6 LYLE "TUNK" TUNKETT: "The Wild Card"

**Archetype starting point:** the unpredictable troublemaker.

**Personality:** Tunk lives on a farm right next to Recall Hill and was raised by his grandpa. He speaks rarely and calmly, and what he says is unsettling. He does feral things (eats things off the ground, keeps a pet raccoon, once drove a combine to school) without explaining them. He isn't malicious; he just operates by rules nobody else knows. The surprise of his character is that every few episodes he says the most emotionally wise thing anyone has said all season, then goes back to licking a battery.

**History and depth:** His parents "went up Recall Hill in 2016 and didn't come back." Nobody knows if they're dead, gone or something stranger. Tunk doesn't talk about it. Grandpa Earl raised him on farm work and old sayings. Tunk has spent his whole childhood playing on a landfill of failed experimental products, which explains a lot.

**Ambition:** Unknown. When asked, he once said, "To be the hill."

**Strengths:** fearless, physically capable, weirdly wise, unbothered by social pressure, can fix anything mechanical.
**Weaknesses:** no impulse control, no sense of personal property, says terrifying things to adults, may be partly radioactive.
**Comedy engine:** unexpected actions, deadpan non sequiturs, and things nobody explains.

**Voice and speech:**
- Talks very little. Often answers a question several scenes later.
- Calm, slow, rural delivery, totally unbothered.
- Says unsettling things in a friendly way: "I buried a thing for you. Don't dig it up till you're thirty."
- Occasionally delivers a perfect, heartfelt line, then ruins it.
- **Voice casting note:** a slow, low, soft-spoken rural boy's voice. Think calm farmhand, not cartoon hick.

**Recurring jokes:** **the Object of the Week**, a different random item he carries all episode without explanation (a traffic cone, a single ski, a framed photo of a stranger) that sometimes pays off at the end; his raccoon **Deposition**; "Grandpa says…" lines that make no sense until the end.

## 4. Character appearance sheets (the locked designs)

> **These descriptions are the canonical model sheets.** Any image prompt, artist brief or AI generation must copy them *word for word*. Changes go through a version bump (v1.1, v1.2…), never through improvisation.

**Base body template (shared by all kids):**
- Head-to-body ratio is about **1 : 1.6**: big heads, short pill-shaped bodies.
- Heads are soft rounded rectangles (not circles; that separates us from other shows).
- Eyes are **large white ovals with small black pupil dots**. There are no irises. Eyebrows are thick, separate floating strokes and do most of the acting.
- Noses are a small rounded "u" shape in a slightly darker skin tone.
- Mouths are simple shapes drawn with an outline, using a 9-shape mouth chart for lip sync.
- Hands are rounded **mittens with four fingers**.
- Legs are short tubes. Shoes are chunky and simplified.
- **Signature outline:** a 4 px warm near-black line (#2B2118), plus a **3 px white "sticker" outer rim** around each character's full silhouette, like a die-cut sticker. This is the show's most recognizable visual detail.

## 5. Personality and dialogue guide: how each one would say the same line

Test: each character reacts to **"The school is replacing lunch with a free AI-generated smoothie."**

| Character | Line |
|---|---|
| **Dex** | "Okay. Huge. This is what I call the Pruitt Principle: food… but faster. Think about it." |
| **Wren** | "Free means we're the product. Again. Incredible." |
| **Marcus** | "Free smoothies means a black market for real lunch. I'm buying every Lunchable in the county by 4 p.m." |
| **Gordy** | "Point one: smoothie. Point two: liquid. Point three: liquids can't be traced. I'm not saying it's connected. I'm saying it's adjacent." |
| **Kenzie** | *(to phone)* "You guys. Not me being the first girl in America to drink lunch." *(phone down, flat)* "What does it taste like. Is it bad. I need it to be bad for engagement." |
| **Tunk** | *(long pause)* "Grandpa says never drink a thing that's thinking." |

**Rules for writing dialogue:**
1. **Each character has a *function* in a scene.** Dex proposes, Wren questions, Marcus prices it, Gordy connects it, Kenzie broadcasts it and Tunk destabilizes it. Don't give a function to the wrong character.
2. **Not everyone gets a joke.** In any given scene, one or two characters land the big laughs and the others react or set them up.
3. **People interrupt.** Use em dashes (—) and overlapping lines. Real friends don't wait their turn.
4. **No one explains the theme.** If a character says the moral out loud, it has to be undercut within three seconds.
5. **Catchphrases at most once per episode per character.** Catchphrases are seasoning, not the meal.
6. **Adults are worse than kids.** The kids are dumb in a childish way; the adults are dumb with power.
7. **Cursing is allowed.** Characters can swear, including strong profanity. It's an adult comedy, and kids this age swear when adults aren't around. Use it like a punchline, not like punctuation:
   - **Each character swears differently.** Dex swears in business-speak ("this is a f***ing paradigm shift"). Wren swears rarely, flatly and with precision, so when she does it lands hardest. Marcus swears about money. Gordy invents half-swears and then commits to a real one at the worst moment. Kenzie censors herself on camera and swears freely off camera (the camera voice/real voice switch). Tunk almost never swears, but when he does it's calm and terrifying.
   - **Adults swear more and worse** (Bev, Mayor Krupp off-mic). Frink never swears, which is creepier.
   - **Running gag:** the town is so "average" that the school's AI filter bleeps random harmless words and lets the real swears through.
   - **Placement:** no strong profanity in the first 7 seconds of any video or Short, and no swearing in titles, thumbnails or descriptions (see the YouTube note in `04-production-workflow.md`).

| Pair | Dynamic | Story fuel |

## 7. Season-long continuity rules
- **Memory is canon.** If something happens on screen, it happened. Embarrassments, debts, bans and injuries carry forward. Track them in `continuity-log.md` (see production workflow).
- **The population sign** is shown at the start of every episode, and its number always reflects the last episode's events.
- **Frink's file on the gang** gets one new note each episode. In the Season 1 finale it is read out in full.
- **Recall Hill accumulates.** Every product that fails in an episode appears on Recall Hill in later episodes.
- **Tunk's Objects of the Week** all show up together in the Season 1 finale (see the season plan).
